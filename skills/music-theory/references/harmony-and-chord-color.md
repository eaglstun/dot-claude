# Harmony and chord color

## Diatonic foundation

In a major key, diatonic triads follow:

| Degree | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii° |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quality | major | minor | minor | major | major | minor | diminished |

In natural minor (Aeolian):

| Degree | i | ii° | III | iv | v | VI | VII |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quality | minor | diminished | major | minor | minor | major | major |

Relative major and minor keys contain the same seven notes but organize them
around different tonal centers. Establish the actual center from phrase endings,
bass emphasis, melody, and dominant motion—not the key signature alone.

## Start with the arrangement

Power chords and triads are clear, direct, and resilient in dense arrangements.
Use them when rhythm, distortion, melody, or lyrics already supply the detail.
Color tones are most audible when the arrangement leaves them room.

The useful question is not “how can this chord be jazzier?” but “what should
this moment feel like, and which note creates that effect?”

## Common chord colors

### sus2 and sus4

Suspensions remove the third, making the harmony neither clearly major nor
minor. A sus2 sounds open and searching; a sus4 carries more tension and often
wants to resolve down to the third. They work well at phrase edges, in
pre-choruses, and anywhere uncertainty is more useful than a firm quality.

### add9

An add9 chord adds the ninth to a triad without requiring a seventh. It adds
light and openness without the weight of a full extended chord. It often works
well on a chorus lift or on a major chord that otherwise feels too blunt.

### Sixth chords

Major-sixth chords are brighter and lighter than major sevenths. They retain the
clarity of a triad while making a cadence feel warmer or more buoyant.

### Major sevenths

The major seventh sits a semitone below the root and creates gentle internal
tension. It reads as lush, floating, or cinematic rather than forceful. It is
often effective in intros, bridges, instrumental spaces, and on IV chords.

### Minor sevenths

Adding the minor seventh softens a minor triad and adds depth without strong
dissonance. It can make a verse or pre-chorus linger rather than land starkly.

### Dominant sevenths

A major triad with a minor seventh contains a tritone between its third and
seventh. That instability gives the chord directional pull, commonly toward a
tonic a perfect fourth above. Use it when a transition needs blues character or
a clearer resolution.

### Extended chords

Ninths, elevenths, altered ninths, and altered thirteenths make the chord more
specific. Introduce them deliberately and check that melody, bass, and voicing
do not obscure or contradict the characteristic tone.

- A natural 9 often adds breadth without much friction.
- An 11 can create modal openness, but may rub against a major third.
- A flat 9 intensifies a dominant and strongly favors resolution.
- A flat 13 adds a dark altered or modal color.

## Slash chords and voice leading

A slash chord specifies a bass note independently from the upper chord. Common
uses:

- **Inversion:** put the third or fifth in the bass to reduce root-to-root jumps.
- **Bass line:** preserve upper harmony while the bass walks by step.
- **Pedal point:** hold one bass note while upper structures change.
- **Ambiguity:** combine a stable bass with an upper chord that implies another
  function or color.

Prefer the option that gives each voice a singable path. Compare successive
chords note by note: retain common tones, move other voices by the smallest
musically useful interval, and let the bass carry larger functional motion when
needed.

## Diminished passing chords

Diminished chords stack minor thirds and are intrinsically unstable. They are
especially effective as chromatic passing chords a semitone below or above a
target chord. Treat them as motion, not a destination, unless the style clearly
supports sustained diminished harmony.

## Borrowed harmony and secondary dominants

Borrowed chords come from a parallel mode, such as using iv or bVII in a major
key. They change color while the original tonic remains intelligible.

A secondary dominant temporarily treats a diatonic chord other than I as a
tonic. Label it by destination—`V/ii`, `V/vi`, and so on—and verify that its
altered notes resolve convincingly. Distinguish this temporary tonicization from
a genuine modulation, which establishes a new tonal center more durably.

## Arrangement heuristics

| Context | Useful starting palette |
| --- | --- |
| Dense chorus or large riff | Triads and power chords |
| Spacious verse | add9, sus2, maj7 |
| Building pre-chorus | sus4 and dominant sevenths |
| Bridge or B section | Extensions and voice-led slash chords |
| Instrumental passage | Wider extensions and pedal harmony |
| Outro or fade | maj7, sixths, and unresolved suspensions |
| Section transition | Inversions, bass lines, or diminished passing chords |

The heavier the rhythm and denser the voicing, the simpler the harmonic label
usually needs to be.

## Quick substitutions

| Starting chord | Possible substitute | Typical effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Imaj7 | Warmer and more floating |
| I | I6 or Iadd9 | Brighter while remaining light |
| IV | IVmaj7 | Lush resolution |
| V | V7 or Vsus4 | Stronger or suspended tension |
| vi | vi7 | Softer, deeper minor color |
| ii | ii7 or ii9/11 | Smooth, open predominant motion |
| Root-position chord | First inversion | Smoother bass movement |
| Two chords a whole step apart | Passing diminished chord | Chromatic approach tension |

Treat this table as a set of hypotheses. Check every substitution against the
melody, bass, register, rhythm, and intended emotional contour.
